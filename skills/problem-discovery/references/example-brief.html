ShiftPlate · Problem Discovery

Should you build ShiftPlate?

Short answer: the problem is real and people already pay to work around it — but ShiftPlate would be entering a crowded, funded race, and its core rule ("we only plan, we never cook") points at the cheap end of the market.

The problem: green light ShiftPlate as designed: needs a rethink

The 30-second version

  • The pain is real. Night-shift workers keep failing to eat well — the same handful of ways, every week.
  • People already pay. $40–$120/wk on takeout and meal kits to paper over it.
  • The money follows the food, not the plan. "Just tell me what to eat" is a near-free commodity.
  • You're not early to an empty space. Funded meal-planning apps ship this exact loop today.
  • Best buyer: the employer. Hospitals and warehouses will pay per-head to keep shift staff fed.

Question 1

Is the pain real? Yes — strongly.

This isn't a few loud people online. There's a recurring, well-documented pattern in shift-worker communities: meals skipped or replaced with vending-machine food, energy crashes mid-shift, weight and sleep problems blamed on erratic eating. Independent write-ups describe the same failure modes over and over: nothing open at 3am, no energy to cook after a 12-hour shift, groceries spoiling on a flipped schedule.

Question 2

Will people pay? Yes — but for the food, not the plan.

Every source points the same way: telling someone what to eat is cheap; getting the food into their hands is where the money is. The category leaders didn't win as "here's a plan" — they won by moving people to "here's the meal, delivered."

ShiftPlate's defining rule — "we only plan, we never touch the food" — plants it on the cheap top rung. That's the core tension.

Question 3

Who actually buys it? The employer — and they'll roll it out.

This is the good news. Employers of large shift workforces (hospitals, warehouses, call centers) already spend on staff wellbeing to cut turnover, and they buy per-head programs, not consumer apps. They're not precious about it — they want something they can offer as a benefit across a whole site.

Question 4

Who else is doing this? A lot of funded people — right now.

This is the part I have to be blunt about. ShiftPlate's "assess → plan → shop → adjust" loop is not an empty category. It's an active space, and several rivals already close the loop and handle the food ShiftPlate refuses to.

The uncomfortable knot

The money is on the food. The competitors handle the food. The "just plan it" lane is a near-free commodity that big recipe and grocery apps already bundle in. ShiftPlate's one firm rule — we never touch the food — sits it squarely in the cheapest, most contested lane, against funded players who go further.

The bottom line

Build it — but not the version in the deck.

The problem is validated; don't second-guess that. What needs to change is the shape. Two moves the evidence is basically shouting:

1
Sell through employers, across whole sites
Not the lone worker who churns in a month. The employer with a shift workforce, a wellbeing budget, and a reason to keep staff fed. Recurring, multi-seat, budget already there.
2
Get closer to the actual meal, or you're a free planner
Make "food in hand on shift, any diet, works with any store" the hero. That's the square the funded rivals (single-diet, own-kitchen) haven't nailed — and it's what people actually pay for.
The one test that settles it

Offer the same 8 employers two things and see which one they put a card down for:

A — "A meal plan for your shift staff, refreshed every week."
B — "We get a good meal into your staff's hands on shift — and show you who's actually eating."

If B wins and they'll pay a premium → build around delivering the meal. If A ≈ B → you're in a price fight, and you should get into the food itself.
