# Artifact Contracts

Read only the section needed for the requested output. Adapt labels to the
domain, active premise, audience, and exact format the user requested.

## Provenance when useful

Do not wrap every artifact in mandatory metadata. Add a compact provenance note
only when it helps the reader judge or update the work. Depending on the
artifact, it may include the active premise, decision or audience, research
date, confidence, consequential unknowns, and linked sources.

Keep these elements in their natural place: sources in slide notes or a research
section, uncertainty beside the affected claim, and the decision in the opening
or conclusion. Exact user-requested formats take precedence.

## Evolving idea brief

Use one brief to preserve a long or changing conversation. Update it in place at
meaningful premise or evidence changes; do not append a transcript or create a
new document per phase.

1. **Aim** — what outcome the user wants
2. **Active premise** — current shape, people, situation, and value mechanism
3. **Relationship** — current-product change, standalone, adjacent, or undecided
4. **What changed** — latest meaningful reframe and what remains valid
5. **Evidence and assumptions** — distinguish observed facts, user claims,
   inference, and repository capability
6. **Open tension** — the uncertainty or trade-off carrying the decision
7. **Next decision** — one choice and what would inform it

Keep it short enough to reread during the conversation. Include current-product
constraints or adoption signals only when they matter to the active premise.

## Opportunity and alternative map

Use to widen the problem or escape premature convergence.

| Route | Person and job | Value mechanism | Why promising | Main risk | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Include the current premise plus meaningfully different routes: narrower user,
adjacent job, service-first/manual, business-model inversion, enabling layer, or
non-product intervention. End with the recommended route and why it fits the
user's aim—not which route sounds largest.

## Research and competitor landscape

Use when the user needs to understand the sea of existing solutions.

1. **Research question and scope** — segment, geography, time window
2. **Market structure** — how buyers currently solve the job
3. **Landscape groups** — direct, adjacent, substitutes, manual/internal,
   non-consumption
4. **Comparison table**

| Alternative | Target user | Core job/workflow | Pricing | Distribution/traction signal | Strength | Weakness/opening | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

5. **Customer pain and voice** — analyze both pain with similar products and
   pain with the underlying situation. Search audience-relevant sources such as
   Reddit, Quora, specialist/developer forums, LinkedIn, X, GitHub issues and
   discussions, app reviews, product communities, and public support boards.
   Summarize recurring themes with linked examples, source/search scope,
   frequency, intensity, observed behavior, segment specificity, recency, and
   counter-signals. Distinguish anecdotal signal from prevalence; do not treat
   posts, likes, upvotes, or viral complaints as market-size evidence.
6. **Failed or changed attempts** — what they teach
7. **Openings** — underserved situations, not merely missing features
8. **Threats** — incumbent response, bundling, switching, trust, regulation
9. **Implications for the active premise**
10. **Unknowns and confidence**

Do not equate funding with customer value, traffic with revenue, or feature gaps
with willingness to switch.

## Assumption and evidence ledger

Use when premises are changing or a decision depends on unproven beliefs.

| ID | Assumption | Type | Importance | Current evidence | Confidence | Cheapest discriminating test | Decision threshold | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Types can include problem, person, behavior, willingness to pay, channel,
solution, feasibility, economics, regulation, and founder fit. Prioritize by
`importance × uncertainty`, not by ease of testing. Record invalidated
assumptions rather than silently deleting them when they explain a premise
change.

## Customer discovery plan

Use for interviews or observed workflow research.

1. **Uncertainty to resolve**
2. **Exact participant profile and exclusion criteria**
3. **Recruiting route and sample rationale**
4. **Questions about past behavior** — triggers, sequence, cost, workarounds,
   purchase decisions; avoid pitching and hypotheticals
5. **Observation or artifact requests** — current tools, screenshots, documents,
   timelines when appropriate
6. **Evidence capture template** — verbatim quote, observed behavior, context,
   interpretation
7. **Decision thresholds** — what strengthens, reshapes, or invalidates the
   premise
8. **Synthesis method** — patterns plus disconfirming cases

Interest, compliments, and willingness to join a waitlist are weak signals
unless paired with behavior or commitment.

## Validation experiment plan

| Assumption | Experiment | Audience | Offer/stimulus | Observable behavior | Pass/reshape/stop threshold | Cost/time cap | Follow-up decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Choose the smallest test that reproduces the risky part of reality. A landing
page does not test retention; an interview does not test payment; a prototype
does not test distribution. State exactly what the experiment cannot prove.

## Positioning and MVP boundary

### Positioning

- best-fit customer and triggering situation
- current alternative
- unique value and proof
- category or frame that helps the customer understand it
- reasons to believe
- who it is intentionally not for

### MVP boundary

- one user, one trigger, one end-to-end job
- riskiest behavior the MVP must expose
- minimum experience needed for credible learning
- included / excluded table with rationale
- manual work allowed behind the scenes
- measurement and stop condition

Do not define MVP as a reduced feature backlog. Define it as the smallest
credible system for learning the decisive behavior.

## Pitch narrative and deck

First define audience, desired decision, stage, and active premise.

Build only the slides the argument needs, usually 8–12:

1. **Opening truth or change** — why attention is warranted
2. **Person and costly situation** — specific pain in context
3. **Existing alternatives fail here** — evidence-backed gap
4. **Product experience** — what the person does and receives
5. **Earned insight / why this works**
6. **Proof** — traction, research, commitments, or clearly labeled early signals
7. **Market entry** — reachable segment and bottom-up logic
8. **Business model** — who pays, for what, and why economics might work
9. **Competition and defensibility** — honest alternatives and wedge
10. **Why now** — verified enabling shift
11. **Team or right-to-win** — relevant evidence, not titles
12. **Ask and milestones** — what the requested support unlocks

For each slide provide:

- **Headline:** a claim, never a topic label
- **Body:** at most three concise points
- **Visual:** the evidence or diagram that makes the claim legible
- **Speaker note:** what to say, including caveats
- **Source:** for any external fact

The headlines read in sequence must form the pitch. Omit irrelevant slides,
reorder around the strongest evidence, and put detailed rebuttals in an
appendix. Never use a giant top-down TAM as a substitute for an entry market.

## Decision memo

Use when the user wants to choose whether or how to proceed without forcing a
binary verdict.

1. decision and deadline
2. user's aim and constraints
3. active premise and alternatives
4. what the evidence says
5. strongest case for the current route
6. strongest disconfirming interpretation
7. irreversible versus reversible risks
8. options with trade-offs
9. recommendation and why
10. next action, signal, threshold, and review date

State uncertainty in the recommendation. A decision memo should make it easier
to disagree intelligently, not close the discussion rhetorically.
