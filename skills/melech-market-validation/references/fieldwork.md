# Primary Discovery and Behavioral Tests

Use this reference after secondary research identifies which claim must meet
reality. Primary discovery explains mechanisms. Behavioral and commercial tests
show whether customers will act.

## Recruit for the hypothesis

Define participants by recent behavior, not demographics alone:

- belong to the target segment
- encountered the triggering situation recently
- personally performed, owned, bought, or approved the current workaround
- match the buyer/user role needed for the active claim

Exclude:

- friends giving encouragement
- people outside the triggering situation
- advisors speaking abstractly for customers
- users when the open claim concerns the economic buyer

Start with a small purposeful sample. Continue until the mechanism repeats,
contradicting patterns become clear, or the declared cap is reached. Do not use
an arbitrary interview count as a validation badge.

## Recruit through credible routes

Depending on the market:

- first-party customers, churned accounts, and lost opportunities
- professional communities and associations
- targeted outbound to people matching behavioral criteria
- partner, agency, or expert referrals
- events, job boards, and workflow-specific communities

Avoid recruiting only from enthusiasts already attracted to the proposed
solution. Record route and selection bias.

## Interview the last real incident

Use a behavior-first sequence:

1. “Tell me about the last time this happened.”
2. “What triggered it?”
3. “What did you do first, then what?”
4. “Who else became involved?”
5. “What failed, slowed down, or became risky?”
6. “What did it cost in time, money, delay, trust, or exposure?”
7. “What have you tried or paid for?”
8. “Why did you keep, abandon, or switch that approach?”
9. “Who owns the outcome and who approves spending?”
10. “What event would make solving this urgent?”

Request artifacts when appropriate: spreadsheets, screenshots, timelines,
tickets, invoices, proposals, or current tools. Observation beats reconstructed
memory when access and privacy permit.

Avoid:

- pitching before understanding behavior
- “Would you use/pay?” hypotheticals
- asking participants to design features
- leading language from the proposed solution
- treating vivid quotes as prevalence

## Capture evidence consistently

For each session record:

- participant fit and recruiting route
- role: user / champion / buyer / approver / blocker
- last incident and date
- trigger, sequence, consequence, and frequency
- current alternative and spend
- switching or adoption barriers
- verbatim evidence
- interpretation, kept separate from the quote
- which claim the evidence supports or contradicts

Synthesize cross-session patterns and disconfirming cases. Do not average away
meaningful segment differences.

## Choose the behavioral test by claim

| Open claim | Better test | What it still cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Problem mechanism | workflow observation, recent-incident interviews | willingness to pay |
| Buyer/budget | concrete proposal, procurement conversation | repeatable acquisition |
| Offer fit | concierge delivery, prototype with consequential action | retention at scale |
| Willingness to pay | deposit, paid pilot, preorder, signed paid proposal | broad market size |
| Reachability | targeted channel test with qualified conversion | product retention |
| Switching readiness | migration/integration/data commitment | long-term satisfaction |

Pick the cheapest test that recreates the risky part of reality. Do not default
to a landing page because it is easy.

## Precommit the experiment

Before launching, write:

- **Claim:** exact belief under test
- **Participant:** behavioral and buyer criteria
- **Offer:** what is promised, delivered, and charged
- **Action:** observable behavior required
- **Threshold:** Advance / Reshape / Stop conditions
- **Cap:** maximum time, participants, and spend
- **Limits:** what a positive result cannot establish

Thresholds depend on context. Use economics and decision risk, not a universal
conversion benchmark.

## Commitment ladder

Weak to strong:

1. compliment or stated interest
2. contact details or waitlist signup
3. scheduled follow-up with the right stakeholder
4. access to workflow, data, or implementation resources
5. signed proposal or letter with concrete obligations
6. deposit, paid pilot, preorder, or purchase
7. repeated use, renewal, expansion, or referral

Interpret commitment relative to friction. A security review may be more
meaningful than a small payment in enterprise software; a deposit may be the
cleaner signal in consumer markets.

## Analyze results

Compare observed behavior with the precommitted threshold. Then ask:

- Did the participant match the target segment?
- Did the action occur for the hypothesized reason?
- Was the buyer involved?
- What selection bias or facilitation inflated the result?
- Which alternative explanation still fits?
- What changed: customer, problem, buyer, offer, price, channel, or timing?

A failed test may invalidate the claim, expose a broken test, or reveal a better
market shape. Distinguish those outcomes rather than calling every failure
“learning.”
