# Research Methods

Read only the section matching the uncertainty currently carrying the decision.
Research should change the map or next move, not create volume.

## Turn claims into questions

Examples:

- "Nobody solves this" → map direct, adjacent, substitute, manual, and internal
  alternatives.
- "People hate the current tools" → find behavioral evidence, switching costs,
  and what users still tolerate or value.
- "The market is growing" → establish the relevant segment, geography, date,
  and credible range rather than citing a giant category TAM.
- "This became possible now" → verify the enabling technical, regulatory, cost,
  or behavioral shift.
- "We can build this easily" → locate operational, data, distribution,
  compliance, and integration bottlenecks.

Choose the claim whose answer most changes the idea. Stop when additional
searches repeat known findings and the next decision is clear.

## Investigate user pain

Use this method only when user pain is a decision-carrying uncertainty. Search
for whichever evidence body is relevant; do not require both every time:

1. **Pain with existing solutions** — complaints, workarounds, cancellations,
   migration stories, missing outcomes, and what users still value.
2. **Pain with the underlying situation** — how people describe the problem
   before naming a product, including consequences, urgency, current behavior,
   and attempts to solve it themselves.

Go where the relevant people already talk: specialized or developer forums,
Reddit, Quora, LinkedIn posts and comments, X threads, GitHub issues and
discussions, app reviews, product communities, and public support boards.
Choose sources based on the audience; GitHub may be high-signal for developers
and irrelevant for restaurant owners.

Useful query shapes:

- `"[problem phrase]" site:[relevant community domain]`
- `"[competitor]" (frustrating OR alternative OR switched OR cancelled)`
- `site:github.com "[workflow or error]" (issues OR discussions)`
- `"[job to be done]" ("how do you" OR workaround OR spreadsheet)`

Assess signal rather than collecting colorful quotes:

- **Frequency** — recurrence across independent people, products, communities,
  and dates
- **Intensity** — inconvenience versus lost time, money, revenue, trust, access,
  or repeated failure
- **Behavior** — workarounds, switching, paying, escalating, or repeatedly
  seeking help
- **Specificity** — fit to the target segment and triggering situation
- **Recency** — persistent, growing, newly enabled, or already resolved
- **Counter-signal** — satisfied users, accepted trade-offs, weak engagement,
  and reasons people do nothing

Report recurring themes, representative links, search scope, and confidence.
Do not turn post counts, likes, upvotes, duplicate reposts, or viral complaints
into prevalence estimates. Public conversation reveals language, mechanisms,
and behavioral signals; it rarely establishes market size.

## Compare the real landscape

Include only categories relevant to the current decision:

- direct competitors serving the same job
- adjacent tools solving part of it
- substitutes and bundled features
- manual workflows, agencies, spreadsheets, and internal builds
- non-consumption: why people accept the problem instead

Compare by user, job, workflow, pricing, distribution or traction signal,
strength, weakness, and why customers stay. A feature gap is not automatically
an opening; explain the market structure, switching behavior, and where the
active premise might enter without copying incumbents.

## Source discipline

1. Start broad enough to learn the market's language.
2. Search named companies, products, pricing, reviews, failure stories, and
   current behavior.
3. Prefer primary sources for product facts and authoritative sources for laws,
   funding, and market data.
4. Use communities and reviews for lived-experience hypotheses, not
   representative statistics.
5. Search for disconfirming evidence and failed attempts.
6. Trace repeated claims back to the original source.

Link important time-sensitive claims and include publication or retrieval dates
when available. Label extrapolation and synthesis as inference. If live research
is unavailable, say so and mark knowledge-based claims for verification.
Absence from search results is not proof that no competitor or demand exists.
